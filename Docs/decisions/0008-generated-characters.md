# 0008: Characters generated with Hunyuan3D, split into the game's parts

**Status:** Accepted (in progress) · 2026-10-18 · supersedes the "not for characters" verdict in the graphics plan's cross-check (rows 6, 8-12 and 15)

## Context

The procedural stand-ins prove the systems (modular assembly, DNA, recolouring, springs, toon shading) but don't look good enough, and the graphics plan's route to good-looking characters (G2: model one character by hand, then a parts catalogue) needs artists the project doesn't have. Hunyuan3D 2.1 now runs locally ([hunyuan3d.md](../hunyuan3d.md)). From one T-pose image it makes a convincing stylised character in about 7 minutes: a clean body, separated fingers on a thumb-and-mitten hand, modelled clothing edges, strand-cut hair, an anime head with eye sockets, and a texture in the right colours.

What it makes is one fused solid with a painted face: no rig, no separate hair or clothes, no recolour regions. The game needs modular parts on one shared rig (decision 0001), recolourable (0002), with body shapes and layering (style bible §3).

## Decision

**Generate whole characters, then decompose them into the game's parts**, conformed to one canonical generated body. The modular system (DNA, the population generator, outfits per context, recolouring, the salon) stays as it is.

1. **The canonical body is generated.** Bald base images (feminine and masculine, T-pose, tight minimal clothing, barefoot) go through Hunyuan3D. Blender scripts then:
   - symmetrise, remesh and smooth it to the PLAN §4 budget (body about 20k triangles plus head 10k);
   - fit the skeleton to it, which **replaces the stand-in skeleton** as the canonical rest pose;
   - weight it automatically and derive the body regions from the weights;
   - author `body.weight`, `body.chest` and `body.hips` as the stand-ins do.

   The masculine scan is wrapped onto the feminine body's topology to make a **`body.femininity` shape**, so one body and one set of parts covers everyone, and `BodyDna.Femininity` finally drives the mesh.
2. **Faces stay the game's own.** The generated head keeps its sculpted shape (eye sockets, nose, chin). The game's face features (eye whites, irises, catch-lights, brows, mouth pieces, blush and smile strokes) are built in Blender against the real head surface and skinned to the face bones, so expressions, blinking, styles and recolouring keep working. Hunyuan3D's painted face is only used to find where the features go.
3. **Hair** comes from generations of the outfit-01 character with that hairstyle, in a colour far from skin and clothes (`Tools/Blender/hairs.json`, `make_generated_hair.py`). The steps are:
   - take the scan's hair-coloured surface, aligned to the canonical body and fitted to its head by the bare face; over the head it's a cap whose edge is the hairline, so the bald head sits inside it like a wig;
   - cover any bare scalp the scan invented (Hunyuan painted the nape under a ponytail as skin), drop hair lying on the chest, and shorten a fringe to the canonical brows so the game's eyes and expressions show;
   - cut it into `hair.front` (in front of the ears, below the crown) and `hair.back`, which meet without a gap;
   - give it the hair mask (base to tip down its length, the highlight ring round the crown), head weights handing over down the spine for long hair, the body's shapes from the head and trunk, and spring chains along hair hanging free behind (a ponytail).

   `GeneratedHairSheet` reviews them (`Docs/review/generated-hair.png`).
4. **Clothing** comes from generations of a character wearing the outfit (same prompt and seed as the base, so the same body), aligned to the canonical body. The steps are:
   - split by colour and body region into the slots (`Tools/Blender/outfits.json` lists each outfit's garments, the regions they may sit on, and the prompt's colours);
   - **fitted garments** (tops, bottoms, legwear, one-pieces, shoes) are **built from the canonical body's own surface** wherever the scan shows the garment, lifted to the layer offset (style bible §3) or to the scan's own looseness, with hollows bridged the way cloth spans them. They inherit the body's weights and every body shape exactly. Pushing the scan's own garment geometry onto the body came out boxy and lumpy, since it kept the outfit model's proportions. Their shape is made in the game's everyday arms-down pose (`Poses.Relaxed`) and unposed to the bind pose, with the slack round each limb evened out and hollows bridged only between parts that move together: shaped in the T-pose, the slack a loose sleeve hangs with under a raised arm bunched into shoulder pads once the arms came down;
   - **loose layers** (coats, and later skirts) keep the scan's geometry, conformed to the body, and can be **settled with Blender cloth simulation** (`--settle`); on a T-posed body it buckles fitted fabric, so it isn't used for those;
   - transfer weights and body shapes to the loose layers;
   - a hood (down, round the neck) stands too far off the body to fit, so it comes from the scan's own geometry and is joined to the fitted garment;
   - build the recolour mask from the generated texture's colour blocks, counting shades of one colour as one block (shading split a burgundy top into blotches), cut along smooth region edges; garment edges are straightened along their own line rather than blurred, so thin straps survive, and a strap's two edges are each cut along their own straight line (cut as one band, a strap about as narrow as the body's triangles came out as a string of beads). A halter's straps rise from the bust to a band round the neck (the scan never shows so thin a strap). A garment the prompt gives one colour gets one region: the rest is shading and stray patches (skin-coloured on a black cocktail dress, purple on a blue gown), which the game would recolour as panels;
   - where the scan can't show a detail, the garment's entry in `outfits.json` gives it: a crew neck's height up the neck and an even collar band, a level hem with turned-up cuffs, shoes' sole and low-top collar line, and drawn details (laces, drawstrings, a fly, pockets) painted as stripes in a recolour region, cut along each straight edge. It can also name regions a garment covers whatever the scan shows (`fill`): tights under a skirt, and a cream satin blouse whose highlights read as skin and left it full of holes;
   - every body shape moves a fitted garment out at least as far as it moves the skin under it (`keep_shapes_clear`): shaped shape by shape, the masculine calves came a few millimetres through tight leggings. And the skin a garment hides is found in the relaxed pose, looking round the limb as well as out along the skin's normal, with the crease between the legs aside: masculine and heavier thighs overlap there, so no cloth can stay outside both, and they came through trousers in patches until trousers hid the thighs under them;
   - tops and bottoms from different outfits mix, so they meet at one waist line (`garment_tools.WAIST_ABOVE_HIPS`, 17.5 cm above the hip joint): every bottom covers the pelvis, reaches the line and stays fitted down to the top of the thigh, and every untucked top reaches 1.5 cm past it. A tucked top's hem sits half a layer in, so any waistband goes over it. Swimwear, lingerie and crop tops opt out (`"waist": false`). `GeneratedMixSheet` (`Docs/review/generated-mix.png`) shows every top over every bottom;
   - outfits generated on the masculine base (`"body": "masculine"`, `Character_00005_`) are measured, and hairstyles built, against the body's masculine shape, then taken back to the basis;
   - a skirt, or a dress's skirt below its fitted bodice, can't be made from the body's surface, which wraps each leg on its own. It is one tube (`garment_tools.build_skirt`): the scan's outline ring by ring, kept clear of both legs in the relaxed pose, weighted to the hips and handing up to half of each side to that thigh. Below the hips it spans the knees, which stand forward in the relaxed pose, instead of tucking back in under them (a cheongsam bulged over them): a flared skirt hangs straight down off its widest point, and a straight one is taut over them and still tapers to its hem. A gown's spec can cap how far it stands off the body (`widest`): a wedding gown's sheer overlay and train scanned as a tent. The tube faces outwards: wound inwards, its front was drawn a second time by the outline pass (cloth is double-sided) at its own depth, and came out in dark blotches and streaks on every skirt and dress. Each ring is also convex, and the hand-over from one thigh to the other goes gradually round it, so the skirt doesn't fold where the legs stand differently from how it was built. The skin it hides is found looking out from the skirt's axis as well as along the skin's normal: along the normal the crotch never met the tube, so the pelvis stayed shown and its outline came through a sundress as black blotches. The legs under it stay shown, so a striding knee doesn't vanish. Under a floor-length gown the scan's legs can't be found, and the guessed joints (feet 20 cm out) dragged the hem in to half the image's width, so such an outfit (`"legs_hidden"`) is aligned with its legs where ours are, moved with the hips;
   - draft the catalog entry: each part's sidecar carries its display name, style tags, lean, contexts and weight from `outfits.json` or `hairs.json` (`Tools/Blender/update_sidecars.py` refreshes them without a rebuild);
   - texture it. Its UVs are in metres of cloth, wrapped round each body part like a tube (`garment_tools.fabric_uvs`): the torso as a front and a back panel joined at the side seams, each arm and leg round its bone with the seam underneath and down the inside leg (a sleeve beginning at the armhole: nothing on the body's side of the shoulder joint is the sleeve's, so stripes run straight up over the shoulders), each measured as true distance round the body, so a texture keeps its size and stripes run round the body and down the limbs (projected from the front, a texture streaked down every side). Each garment is made of a **fabric** (`outfits.json` "fabric"): its material's base map is that fabric's surface, a seamless grey tile of its weave, stitches or grain shading the region colours, brightened by the tile's mean so the cloth keeps its colour, and lifted: the shader also adds the structure as light, the more the darker the cloth (`_BaseLift`, the fabric's "lift"), since darkening alone doesn't show on black, so a black knit shows its stitches and black patent its shine. Shiny cloth also has gloss (`_Gloss`, the fabric's "gloss"): a hard-edged toon highlight where it mirrors the main light toward the eye, small and bright on patent and vinyl, broader and softer on satin and lycra; and pile has sheen (`_Sheen`), a soft glow toward its edges, so velvet, chenille and faux fur look like themselves. Open fabrics (lace, mesh, crochet, eyelet, guipure, fishnet, ripped denim) mark their holes in their texture's alpha, and the wearer's skin shows through them (`_Sheer`): wholly on lingerie, swimwear and legwear (fishnet tights), half on other clothes, as if lined. Tights and stockings come in fishnet, lace or a sheen too, socks in a plain knit or fishnet. Structures are drawn bigger than life, as a cel-shaded game draws them (a knit stitch 14 mm, a corduroy wale 12 mm), each with a broader layer (a denim's fade, a tweed's flecks, a satin's sheen) so it reads across a room. The fabric library (`Tools/Textures/fabrics.py`, 98 fabrics: cottons and linens, sateen and dotted swiss, twills and denim in three washes, black and ripped (the skin shows through its rips), performance knit, tricot and honeycomb sports mesh, ribbed and shimmer swim lycra, suiting wools, corduroys from needlecord to jumbo cord, scuba, knits from jersey and marl to cable, mohair and pointelle, satin, shantung, moiré, taffeta, brocade, plissé, velvet and crushed velvet, lace, sequins, glitter, foil lamé and beading, leathers, vinyl and snakeskin, quilting and glossy puffer, guipure, straw, patchwork, pleats and smocking...) and the **print** library (`prints.py`, 218 prints: stripes, checks, tartans and glen plaid, triple stripes, regimental stripes, quilt blocks, painted spots, dots and halftones, florals from a dense small floral to poppies and lavender, fruit and botanicals, geometrics from seigaiha and shippo to ikat, damask and art deco fans, a bandana, botanical sketches, cute, nautical, seasonal, gothic and street motifs, animal prints, shibori, batik and watercolour, newsprint, handwritten script, a toile of birds on branches, groovy waves, flower power, Memphis, graffiti, speed swooshes, thorny vines, skeleton keys, gold chains, rosebuds, dinosaurs, dogs, frogs, sailboats, pineapples and autumn leaves) are drawn procedurally by `Tools/Textures/make_textures.py`. A print's red channel is how much of the pattern colour shows, its green channel ink (outlines and shading drawn in the print) and its blue channel its second colour, which follows the pattern colour by the print's rule (`PatternEntry.Accent`): a natural green for a floral's leaves, a lemon's or a watermelon's rind, gold for a daisy's heart or a cone, the print colour paled for a toadstool's stem, its opposite hue for a planet's rings. A repeated print is drawn finer on shoes and legwear (leopard spots sized for a dress were huge on a pump), and starts at an offset of its own for each wearer (from their seed and the item), so two people in the same floral don't wear it the same. Some prints are placed once rather than repeated (`PatternEntry.Placement`): "chest", graphic tees (a heart, a skull, a retro sunset, a cat's face, a ghost, a crown, slogans in a pixel font, a varsity number... 32 of them), one 20 cm motif round the front centre line at bust height, only on a casual top or dress; "badge", an 11 cm emblem over the left breast of any top or dress, as on a polo; "back", a 24 cm graphic between the shoulder blades of a casual top, dress or jacket; and "body", a dye over the whole garment from its top to its hem (ombre and reverse ombre, dip-dye, sunset fade, colour block, hem stripes), which spans each garment's own height (`PartEntry.ClothBottom`/`ClothTop`, measured from its fabric UVs when the content is written); and bands that repeat round a garment but sit at one height, "hem" just above the hem (a border of flowers, a scalloped lace edge, a Greek key, folk embroidery, ric-rac) and "yoke" under the neckline (an embroidered yoke). The fabric UVs make these fixed places on every garment (a graphic's scale sizes it, 0.8 to 1.25 of its place, so they differ from wearer to wearer): they're metres, u from the front centre towards the wearer's left, the back panel's u from 10 m, each sleeve's and foot's from its own offset beyond (`LIMB_U`), and each trouser leg's lined up with the hips' down the front of the thigh, so a vertical stripe or a check carries on across the seam where the leg meets the body; and v is the height, down a sleeve or a trouser leg its root's height less the distance along it, so a stripe round the hips meets itself round the thighs. `FabricTextures` makes a material per fabric, which `CharacterModelImporter` gives each part by its sidecar. Each garment can also be made in other fabrics (`outfits.json` "fabrics", the catalog part's fabrics): the generator makes about a third of items in one of them, weighted by style (leather for street, tweed for bookish, velvet for gothic), and prints only go on fabrics that take them (not lace, sequins, leather or a cable knit); a knit or a rib takes only knitted prints (Fair Isle, Nordic stars, a Christmas jumper's reindeer, knitted hearts and diamonds, a snowflake yoke, stripes, argyle: the fabric's print tags, `ContentCatalog.PrintsFor`). Tights, stockings and knee socks take prints too (polka-dot tights, striped socks). Fabrics and prints also carry the occasions they suit (sleep, swim, formal, rain), which the outfit's context weights, so pyjamas come in flannel and waffle, clouds and bunnies, a gala gown in velvet or taffeta, swimwear in tropical leaves and stripes, a gala dress in damask, a rainy day in raindrops, umbrellas and checks under waxed cotton or vinyl. Pyjamas, bikinis and lingerie are mostly matching sets, plain or printed, and now and then a casual outfit with a printed top is a co-ord: the top's colour, and its print where the bottom takes it, carried on to the bottom (the one exception to tops and bottoms always contrasting). A print is now and then tone on tone, a deeper or paler shade of the cloth's own colour, more often for elegant styles. Many prints have classic colourways (`PatternEntry.Colourways`: leopard in tan and black, zebra in white and black, navy Breton stripes on white, red and black buffalo check, a Christmas jumper in red and white), which an item with the print takes now and then, where the ground stands apart from the skin. Leather, suede and patent take only animal prints, so shoes come in leopard, zebra or snakeskin now and then (less often than other items take a print). Some fabrics come in their own colours more often than not (the library's "colours" and "colourChance"): denim indigo, black or grey, straw natural, leather black and browns, suede tan, so an item made in one is usually dyed one of them, where it still stands apart from the skin and the rest of the outfit. `GeneratedTextureSheet` shows every fabric and print on a dress and a shirt (`Docs/review/generated-fabrics.png`, `generated-prints.png`), and legwear and shoes in their fabrics and prints (`generated-legwear.png`).

   The physics runs at authoring time. In game, motion stays on spring bones: runtime cloth for 40 residents doesn't fit decision 0004's budget. `GeneratedOutfitSheet` reviews the parts on the generated body (`Docs/review/generated-outfit.png`).
5. **Everything arrives through decision 0005's path:** FBX plus a sidecar, through `CharacterModelImporter`, checked by the Blender validator, reviewed on the part sheet, the 96-character quality sheet and the lineups.
6. **The game runs on either content.** `GeneratedContent` (Game › Characters › Content › Write Generated Content) bakes `Content/GeneratedContent.asset` and `Content/GeneratedCatalog.json` from the generated body and the sidecars. The catalog is the stand-in catalog's sliders, styles, palettes, archetypes and contexts with the generated parts, minus whatever they can't serve, so it validates and generation never fails. Today that leaves out face marks. It adds swimming and sleepwear occasions, barefoot, dressed only from parts tagged for them, so swimwear and lingerie never turn up in everyday outfits, and a formal one (gowns, a wedding dress, a jumpsuit; for men a dress shirt and trousers). Uniforms (scrubs) join the work clothes. The town dresses for them, and copes without them: `SimWorld.OutfitAt` only picks an outfit the catalog has. With the generated content, people are in nightwear at home at night (22:00 to 9:00, so the player wakes in it), in swimwear at the beach (Sandpiper Cove, on dry days), and in formal wear at the town's occasional events (`TownEvents`, the catalog's "events": the Harbour Gala at The Night Owl some Friday or Saturday nights, a garden party in the park some weekend afternoons, called off in the rain), which draw everyone who's out and not at work. On the stand-ins there are no events, and the beach is in everyday clothes. `TownCapture.RunOccasions` shows all three in the town (Docs/review/town-night-home.png, town-beach.png, town-gala.png). Spring colliders are re-expressed in the generated bones' axes. `GameContent` (Game › Characters › Content › Game Uses …) switches the main menu, town and creator between the two. Since 2026-10-22 the game runs on the generated content by default; the stand-ins are still what the tests and most review sheets use, and one menu item switches back. A look saved on the stand-ins loads through `DnaRepair`, which keeps the person and re-dresses them from the same seed.

The images are text-to-image, not edits of one base, so no two generations share a body exactly. Aligning and conforming to the canonical body absorbs that. If it doesn't, Hunyuan3D-Omni can condition a generation on the canonical body's point cloud.

## Consequences

- **Good:** the look comes from generated sculpts instead of capsules, while every system built on the stand-ins keeps working. New hair and outfits cost an image and a script run, not modelling time. The whole game loop (menu, creator, town, save, continue) runs on generated content (`Game.Town.FlowCheck`, `Docs/review/menu-generated.png`). In the town benchmark it renders faster than the stand-ins (8.1 against 11.1 ms, `Docs/review/perf-generated.md` and `perf.md`). It has 3.3 times the vertices and 1.85 times the triangles but fewer renderers (227 against 290), as draw calls are the cost. The comparison flatters it, though: generated residents wear no accessories or face marks yet.
- **Cost:**
  - Replacing the canonical skeleton moves every joint. The 70 procedural stand-ins are built in absolute coordinates, so they misfit until they are retargeted or replaced by generated parts.
  - The PipelineTest FBXs, `character_tools.SKELETON`, spring colliders and the hard-coded head centre and radius all follow the new skeleton.
  - Generated content is the default without full coverage: 134 garments from 68 outfits (four of them men's: the women's run from lingerie and swimwear to gowns, the men's are mostly everyday, swim and sleep) and 22 hair pieces from 11 styles (four men's), against 70 stand-in parts in 17 slots. Nothing generated exists yet for accessories or face marks, and men have no formal wear beyond a shirt and trousers. A knee-length trench coat couldn't be built (the loose-layer path tore it apart; outer layers are built like tops, so they stop at the hips).
- **Risks:**
  - Generated hands are mittens with a thumb.
  - Separate generations may not align well enough to conform.
  - The mix sheet (`generated-mix.png`, every top over every bottom) is now 9360 by 9600 pixels and should be split.
  - Pale garments (white, cream, pastel pink, gold) read as skin in the scan and come out with holes unless their spec fills them in, and bright prints and sequins aren't kept: one-colour garments come out solid (a plaid nightshirt is plain red). A garment's spec has to follow its image, not its prompt: text-to-image changed a fair number of them (cropped tops, a halter instead of a bandeau, a column gown instead of a full skirt).
  - `GeneratedLineup.RenderGeneratedContent` (Docs/review/generated-lineup-<occasion>.png) shows a town dressed from the generated content for every occasion, where problems are easy to see. A belted garment's belt colour isn't a colour block of its own (taken as one, the slim trousers' shading came out in belt-coloured patches down the legs). Short shorts take their hem from the spec (the scan's dipped into a notch at the inner thigh), a jacket can stop at a level hem without being tucked in (`length`), flared trousers keep their width to the edge (the edge blended back to the skin's offset, gathering them like harem trousers), and a one-shoulder piece has its one strap (`one_shoulder`). Tall boots exclude ankle-length trousers (rule tag `long_legs`): both sit one layer off the skin, so the shafts came through the trouser legs; jeans cut to tuck into boots aren't tagged.
  - Text-to-image doesn't always keep the T-pose: a wedding gown came out with the arms hanging (A-pose), and the arms landed on the hips and thighs and swelled its skirt. Such an image needs another seed.
  - Generated texture quality limits the recolour masks. Hunyuan invents the views it can't see: it painted a bald head's crown dark grey and a skin-coloured seam down each side of a pair of shorts. Skin is read from the face and hands only, and garment coverage is closed over gaps a few centimetres wide.
  - The Tencent licence doesn't cover the EU, UK or South Korea; the user has accepted that for now.
- **Graphics plan:** G2's "one hand-modelled character" becomes "one generated character at the bar"; G3's parts catalogue is generated. G1 (shader parity) is unchanged and applies to generated parts as it does to stand-ins.
